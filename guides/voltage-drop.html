What Is Voltage Drop?

The hidden loss in long cable runs

When current flows through a cable, the cable's resistance uses up some of the voltage along the way. This is called voltage drop. It increases with distance, with higher current, and with thinner cable.

A simple formula

For a single-phase circuit, approximately:

Voltage drop = 2 × Current × Cable resistance (Ω/km) × Length (km)

The factor of 2 is needed because current travels out on one conductor and back on the other.

Worked example

Take a 2.5 mm² copper cable (about 7.41 Ω/km), 30 m long, carrying 10 A.

Length = 0.03 km, so the drop = 2 × 10 × 7.41 × 0.03 ≈ 4.4 V. On a 230 V supply, that is about 1.9%.

How much drop is acceptable?

As general guidance, a drop of around 3% is commonly accepted for lighting circuits, and around 5% for other circuits. Higher drop makes lights dim, causes motors to run hot, and shortens the life of pumps and compressors.

If the drop is too high on a long run, use a larger cable or split the circuit into two shorter ones. This is often cheaper and more effective.
